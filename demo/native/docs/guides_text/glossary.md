# Racing glossary

### Apex
The point where the car comes closest to the inside of a corner. A wider arc allows more speed: double the radius and
the speed rises by about 1.4 times. The fastest line is usually a **late apex**: a slower, tighter entry that lets you
accelerate earlier onto the straight. It's also safer, because you have time to correct a mistake. Apex later the
tighter the corner, the more power you have, the less grip there is and the less you know the track. When in doubt,
apex late.

### Brake bias
How the braking force is split between front and rear. Too far back and the car spins even in a straight line; too far
forward and you need longer to stop. Change it in practice, not in a race.

### Corner types
- **Slow in, fast out:** a corner before a long straight. The most important kind: late apex, exact minimum speed.
- **Fast in, slow out:** a straight into a slower section. Carry speed as long as you can and brake late; the exit
  suffers, on purpose.
- **Compromise:** no long straight either side, often in a sequence. Give up the exit of one corner to set up the next.
- **Hairpin:** one very late apex, with the car turned early. **Long bend:** two apexes, the first on the brakes, the
  second on the throttle.
- **Chicane:** smooth, little steering in the middle, favour the exit. **Esses:** give up each exit for the next entry,
  usually without braking.
- **Opening corner** (gets wider): get the slow part done, then power early. **Tightening corner:** brake deep, or treat
  it as fast in, slow out.

### Elevation and camber
Crests unload the car, so don't stamp on the throttle over them. Downhill corners oversteer, and lifting there can snap
the rear round: slow down before the corner. Uphill corners understeer; slowing down brings the steering back.
**On-camber** corners (sloped toward the inside) are grippier than they look; **off-camber** ones are slipperier.

### Grip
How hard the tyres can push on the road. More grip means more cornering speed. A wet road, dirt or two wheels on the
grass cost a lot of it. Going off: **unwind the steering**. Past the limit, more lock doesn't turn the car any more.
Rejoin with as little steering as you can, and check your mirrors first.

### Heel and toe
Braking and blipping the throttle with the same foot while you change down: ball of the foot on the brake, the side of
it rolling onto the throttle. Blip and change down at the end of the braking zone; earlier risks over-revving. If you
can't, finish braking, then blip and change. A slipped clutch is better than a dumped one, which can lock the rears.

### Minimum speed point
The slowest point of a corner, usually where your foot moves from brake to throttle. Every corner has one best speed,
position and angle there. In traffic, keep it simple: get to the slowest point, then away from it. After it you should
only add throttle and unwind the steering; if you have to lift or add lock, something went wrong earlier.
- **Too fast:** unwind a little, pick a later slowest point, delay the throttle.
- **Too slow:** don't add throttle early (you'll run wide and lift twice). Keep a little extra lock and shorten the
  exit.

### Oversteer
The rear slides more than the front. A little is fast; uncontrolled, it causes most spins. Common causes:
- braking mid-corner (straighten the car first if you must);
- lifting off mid-corner, which can usefully turn the car, or spin it;
- too much power: spinning rear tyres lose grip;
- changing down mid-corner, or a sudden clutch;
- kerbs, painted lines and bumps.

Catch it with the throttle or by unwinding the steering.

### Passing
The first car to turn in owns the corner, unless the other car got alongside in the braking zone. Settle it under
braking, not mid-corner. The overtaking car must make the pass clean. Let a much faster car by quickly, and leave a car
width or two. You get **one** defensive move; more is blocking. Don't dive into a corner the other car has already
turned into, and give a slower car room on the exit.

### Slip angle
The difference between where a tyre points and where it actually goes. A tyre grips best with a little slip; grip falls
away faster below the best slip than above it, so err toward more. The fast way round is a mild four-wheel slide, not a
drift. If the car feels like it's on rails, you're probably too slow.

### Threshold braking
Braking as hard as possible without locking. Reach full pressure quickly, then ease off. Ramping up gently is slow,
and the long braking zone invites a car to dive past.

### Trail braking
Releasing the brake gradually as you turn in. Most of the slowing still happens in a straight line. It lets you brake
later and turn the car earlier, and the steering's weight tells you how close to the limit you are. To learn it, brake
more gently, release smoothly, and add a little steering just before the brake is fully off.

### Understeer
The front slides: you turn the wheel and the car goes straight on. Causes and cures:
- **Too much brake:** release some to get the steering back.
- **Too much power:** accelerating takes weight off the front; ease off.
- **Too much lock:** past the best slip angle, more steering makes it worse; slow down or unwind.
- **Uphill:** the weight moves back; straighten the car, keep a steady throttle.

### Using the replay
Compare your lap with a faster one corner by corner: where you brake, the slowest speed and where it is, and where you
are back on full throttle. Ask why at every gap, try one change at a time, and watch the lap time.
